## At a glance

Elite4print is a print-production commerce platform. It had been in build for roughly six months before I joined the company, and I took it over some months after that — so unlike every other project in this portfolio, I did not set the scope, choose the stack, or run the kickoff.

This case study is about the other PM skill: picking up something already moving and making it deliverable.

## The problem

Taking over a live project is a different job from starting one. The codebase encodes decisions you were not part of, the client has a working relationship with someone who is leaving, and there is usually no document that explains why things are the way they are.

The temptation is to rewrite — to reach for the familiar by re-specifying everything. That is almost always the wrong call: it spends the client's money re-litigating settled decisions and resets a delivery timeline that was already in progress.

## My role

Project Manager, inherited mid-flight:

- **Codebase and scope orientation** — understanding a system and a specification I had not written.
- **Client relationship** — becoming the single point of contact through the handover.
- **Quality** — running QA on an existing build and driving the defect list to closure.
- **Team management** — managing the developers day to day.
- **Requirements** — translating incoming change requests into written feature requirements.

## Approach

<Figure
  src="/case-studies/elite4print/screen-3.webp"
  alt="Elite4print order and production workflow screen"
  caption="The production workflow I inherited, ran QA on, and stabilised."
/>

**Read before changing anything.** The first work was not planning, it was reading — the code, the existing tickets, and the client correspondence, until I could explain the current state without asking anyone. A PM who needs a developer to translate the system cannot make credible commitments about it.

**Stabilise quality first.** An inherited build with an open defect list has no reliable baseline. Running QA myself and closing out defects came before new feature work, because until the current state is known, every estimate is a guess.

**Become the single point of contact quickly.** Handover is the moment a client is most likely to lose confidence. I moved to being the one person they contacted, rather than letting communication run through two people during the transition.

**Write the change requests down.** Requests arriving mid-project as conversation are the fastest route to disputed scope. Every incoming change became a written feature requirement, which made scope decisions explicit rather than remembered.

- A stabilised defect position on an inherited build.

## Outcome

- The project continued delivering through a PM change without a reset of scope or timeline.
- Client communication consolidated to one owner during the period it was most fragile.
- Change requests moved from conversation into written requirements, so scope stayed explicit.

## What I'd do differently

Write the takeover document I wished existed. I reconstructed context by reading; I did not then write that context down for whoever comes next. The handover documentation I now produce as standard on every project came directly out of this experience — but Elite4print itself did not get it at the point I arrived.
