## At a glance

Thrll is a personalised adventure platform — a mobile app that matches people to activities and experiences based on what they actually want rather than what is nearest.

I ran it as Project Manager from kickoff to handover with a team of three, on the legacy delivery model, adapted for a mobile release cycle.

## The problem

<Figure
  src="/case-studies/thrll/screen-1.webp"
  alt="Thrll mobile app onboarding asking the user to pick an adventure style"
  caption="Matching starts with signal, not with a filter panel."
/>

Discovery apps in the experiences space tend to fail in the same way: they optimise for inventory breadth and leave the user to filter. The result is a catalogue, and a catalogue puts the work back on the person who came looking for a recommendation.

Personalisation is the obvious answer and the hard one, because it needs signal about preference before the user has given you much. The product question was how to get useful matching out of thin early data without an onboarding flow so long that people abandon it.

## My role

Project Manager end-to-end, managing three team members:

- **Requirements and specification** — requirement gathering, user stories, PRD and SRS.
- **Technical and design direction** — technical decisions, design requirements and guidelines, and the UI/UX review loop.
- **Delivery process** — Agile/Scrum with daily standups, sprint planning, sprint review, and retrospectives.
- **Client communication** — weekly client meetings and daily written updates.
- **Quality** — wrote the test cases and ran the SQA pass.
- **Release** — deployment plan, app-store submission, and handover documentation.

## Approach

<Figure
  src="/case-studies/thrll/screen-3.webp"
  alt="Thrll personalised recommendations feed after a short onboarding"
  caption="A fixed onboarding budget feeding a personalised feed."
/>

**Budget the onboarding.** Every question asked before a user sees value is a chance to lose them. I specified onboarding as a fixed budget of interactions rather than an open list of preferences to collect, which forced the team to decide which signals genuinely improved matching.

**Mobile changes the release plan, not the process.** The Scrum cadence stayed identical, but the schedule accounted for store review as a dependency with its own lead time. Planning submission as a work item rather than an afterthought is the difference between a launch date and a hope.

**Legacy model, three people.** This ran the three-month cycle. Documented the same way as every other project — PRD, SRS, test cases, deployment plan, handover — so the client was not dependent on the team's memory after delivery.

## Outcome

- Delivered and handed over with complete documentation.
- App-store submission planned into the schedule rather than retrofitted.
- Another data point in the legacy-versus-AI-native comparison: three people on a three-month cycle for scope that the newer workflow would compress.

## What I'd do differently

Define how the matching gets evaluated before building it. Personalisation is easy to ship and hard to assess — without an agreed measure of a good match, "is this working" becomes a subjective conversation between client and team. I would set that measure during specification now.
