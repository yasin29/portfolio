## At a glance

Ktalk is a Korean language-learning platform. I took it over as **Product Manager** rather than Project Manager — which meant owning what the product should be, not only whether it shipped on time.

## The problem

  src="/case-studies/ktalk/screen-1.webp"

  caption="Taken over mid-flight, with product direction owned from that point."
/>

Language-learning products have no shortage of content and a chronic shortage of retention. The hard problem is not teaching Korean; it is being the thing a learner opens on the day they do not feel like studying.

Taking the product over mid-flight added a second problem: inheriting a roadmap built on assumptions I had not been part of forming. Some of those assumptions were sound, and the job was to work out which.

## My role

Product Manager, inherited mid-flight:

- **Product direction** — owning what got built and what did not, not just the schedule.
- **Requirements** — translating direction into written requirements the team could build against.
- **Delivery cycle** — running the requirement-to-release loop through to delivery.
- **Stakeholder communication** — representing the product to the client.

## Approach

**Separate inherited decisions from inherited assumptions.** A decision that was made deliberately and a habit that nobody ever questioned look identical in a codebase. Sorting one from the other was the first product work, because it determined what was safe to change.

**Own direction, not just dates.** The distinction between Project Manager and Product Manager is whether you are accountable for the plan or for the product. Here it was the product, which meant saying no to requests that would have shipped fine but pulled the product sideways.

**Keep the delivery process intact.** The PM discipline did not change because the title did — written requirements, a real release cycle, and stakeholder communication on a fixed cadence.

## What shipped

- Continued releases against written requirements after the product changed hands.
- A product direction owned by one person rather than distributed across a transition.

## Outcome

- Delivered through a mid-flight product-owner change without a roadmap reset.
- One of four products where I held Product Manager rather than Project Manager responsibility — the group that shows ownership of *what* to build, not only *how* it gets delivered.

## What I'd do differently

Interrogate the retention model earlier. I focused first on stabilising direction and delivery, which was the right sequence for a handover, but retention is the metric that decides whether a learning product works. That question deserved attention sooner than it got it.

## Stack & tools

**Delivery:** Written requirements · release cycle management · stakeholder communication · Agile/Scrum
