## At a glance

Bside is a mobile art-exhibition app with AI artwork recognition: point a camera at a piece and get the work, the artist, and the context behind it. It shipped to both the App Store and Google Play.

I ran it as Project Manager with a team of three in total — one designer, one mobile developer, and me. On this project I did design work and fixed issues directly alongside running delivery, and I owned app-store submission for both platforms.

## The problem

<Figure
  src="/case-studies/bside/screen-1.webp"
  alt="Bside mobile app home screen showing the artwork feed"
  caption="The app opens on the feed — recognition is one tap away."
/>

Standing in front of a piece of art, the information you want is immediate and the patience you have is short. A gallery placard gives you a title; a search engine gives you fifty results for the wrong painting. The gap is recognition — turning a phone camera into the thing that identifies the work in front of you.

That puts a hard technical dependency at the centre of the product. A recognition pipeline that is accurate but slow fails the moment of use just as completely as one that is fast and wrong. Everything else in the app is downstream of getting that trade-off right.

## My role

Project Manager end-to-end, on a team of three:

- **Requirements and specification** — requirement gathering, user stories, PRD and SRS.
- **Technical direction** — technical decisions across the mobile client and the recognition service.
- **Design** — wrote the design requirements and guidelines, and contributed design work directly rather than only reviewing it.
- **Delivery process** — Agile/Scrum with daily standups, sprint planning, sprint review, and retrospectives.
- **Client communication** — weekly client meetings and daily written updates.
- **Quality and fixes** — wrote the test cases, ran the SQA pass, and fixed issues hands-on.
- **Release** — deployment plan, **app-store submission for iOS and Android**, and handover documentation.

## Approach

<Figure
  src="/case-studies/bside/screen-3.webp"
  alt="Bside artwork detail view showing the recognised work and its artist context"
  caption="Recognition result — the work, the artist, and the context behind it."
/>

**Separate the recognition service from the app.** The mobile client and the PyTorch recognition pipeline were built as separate services, with the model served from AWS ECS. That let recognition work iterate on its own cadence without blocking the app, and kept the client thin enough to ship to two stores on schedule.

**Three people meant no handoff slack.** With one designer and one developer, there is no room for a specification that needs interpreting. Written user stories, a PRD, and an SRS mattered more on a small team, not less — the documentation is what let three people work in parallel rather than in sequence.

**Do the work where it is faster to do it.** Where a design tweak or a defect fix was quicker for me to make directly than to specify, escalate, and re-review, I made it. That is a judgement call rather than a policy, and it works because I read the code — but on a three-person team it removed real round-trip time.

**Store submission is a project, not a step.** Both stores have review requirements that are cheaper to design for than to retrofit, so submission went into the schedule rather than being treated as what happens after "done".

## Outcome

- Live on both app stores at [bside.art](https://www.bside.art/), shipped by a team of three.
- Delivered on the AI-native approach, carrying more scope per person than the legacy model allowed.
- Both store submissions cleared review without a rejection cycle, because requirements were designed in rather than patched afterwards.

## What I'd do differently

Set an explicit accuracy and latency bar for the recognition model before build, and test against it continuously. We evaluated recognition quality as it improved, which was workable, but a written threshold — this confidence level, under this many seconds — would have made "is it good enough to ship" a measurement rather than a conversation.
