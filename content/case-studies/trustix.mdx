## At a glance

Trustix is a supplier-management dashboard for sports-ticket operations — the internal system a ticket business uses to keep suppliers, inventory, and fulfilment in one place instead of across inboxes and spreadsheets.

I ran it as Project Manager from kickoff to handover with a team of four, on the legacy delivery model.

## The problem

<Figure
  src="/case-studies/trustix/screen-1.webp"
  alt="Trustix supplier-management dashboard overview showing supplier and inventory state"
  caption="Supplier state made observable rather than remembered."
/>

Sports-ticket supply is a fast, high-variance market: inventory changes value by the hour, suppliers each have their own terms, and a mistake is expensive in a way that is immediately visible to a customer holding a ticket that does not work.

Operations teams in this position usually run on spreadsheets plus relationships. That works until volume rises, and then the failure mode is not dramatic — it is a slow accumulation of small reconciliation errors nobody has time to chase. The dashboard's job was to make supplier state observable rather than remembered.

## My role

Project Manager end-to-end, managing four team members:

- **Requirements and specification** — requirement gathering, user stories, PRD and SRS.
- **Technical and design direction** — technical decisions, design requirements and guidelines, and the UI/UX review loop.
- **Delivery process** — Agile/Scrum with daily standups, sprint planning, sprint review, and retrospectives.
- **Client communication** — weekly client meetings and daily written updates.
- **Quality** — wrote the test cases and ran the SQA pass.
- **Release** — deployment plan and handover documentation through to delivery.

## Approach

<Figure
  src="/case-studies/trustix/screen-3.webp"
  alt="Trustix supplier detail view with terms and fulfilment history"
  caption="The specification followed the operator day, so high-frequency paths got the attention."
/>

**Start from the operator's day, not the data model.** For an internal tool, the specification that matters is the sequence of things a person actually does between opening the dashboard and closing it. I wrote the user stories from that sequence, which kept the build focused on reducing clicks in the high-frequency paths rather than covering every theoretical case.

**Treat supplier terms as data, not as code.** Each supplier has different arrangements. Encoding those as configuration rather than special-case logic was the technical decision with the longest tail — it is what determines whether adding a supplier later is a form entry or a development ticket.

**Four people on the legacy model.** This project ran the three-month cycle with a full team. It is the useful contrast to Artlive and Bside: identical PM process, meaningfully more people for the scope delivered, because the AI-native workflow was not yet in place here.

- Full delivery artefacts: PRD, SRS, test cases, deployment plan, and handover documentation.

## Outcome

- Delivered to the client and handed over with complete documentation.
- Supplier state moved from spreadsheets and inboxes into one observable system.
- Delivered with **four people on the legacy three-month model** — the baseline the AI-native projects are measured against.

## What I'd do differently

Instrument the dashboard from day one. We built the screens an operations team needs, but without usage data the question "which of these views actually gets used" stays a matter of opinion. Basic event tracking would have made the second phase of prioritisation evidence-led.
